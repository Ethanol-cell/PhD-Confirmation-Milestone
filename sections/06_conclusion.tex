\section{Progress III: J1 Geometry Distillation}\label{sec:j1}

\subsection{From explanation to transferable geometry}

The third stage asks whether representation geometry has practical training
value. Classical output knowledge distillation (KD) tells a student which classes the teacher considers
similar, but it does not specify how spatial organization develops. Direct
feature regression, conversely, may force a compact student to reproduce
teacher channels or transitions that its architecture cannot efficiently
realize. \jmethod{} adopts a selective principle: organize space like the
teacher at a useful early state, permit a student-specific bridge, constrain
balanced coordinate geometry at selected transition and late states, and match
relational evolution after semantic maturity.

\textbf{The transition taps were selected empirically rather than assumed.}
Earlier experiments tested supervision at early, transition, and late states.
Early relational guidance was useful, but applying coordinate or flow
constraints too early, extending them continuously through the full hierarchy,
or relying only on the most compressed late state performed worse than the
selective transition-centred configuration. The final design therefore anchors
E1, leaves the E1-to-M1 bridge student-specific, applies coordinate supervision
at M1/M2 where spatial organization is becoming semantic, and uses L1/L2 mainly
to constrain mature geometry and late relational flow \cite{chan2026j1}.

The production implementation has undergone a separate implementation-forensics
audit, conducted independently of the training-code execution used to produce
the benchmark \cite{chan2026j1}. The audit reproduced descriptors, scalar losses, gradients,
one-step updates, and deployment logits to numerical precision. It corrected
the written specification of the spatial weights, teacher gate, shared
schedule, and hard-sign conventions without identifying a scientific code
error.

\subsection{Verified total objective}

For epoch $e$, the verified loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{J1}}(e)
=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+6\mathcal{L}_{G}
+m(e)\left[
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}\gamma_\ell
\mathcal{L}_{J32}^{(\ell)}
+\lambda_E\mathcal{L}_{E1}
+\lambda_F\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}
\right],
\label{eq:j1-total}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}=\{M1,M2,L1,L2\}$. The terms encode different knowledge
objects rather than repeated versions of one feature loss.

\paragraph{Step 1: aligned input and supervised classification.}
For image-label pair $(x_i,y_i)$, one stochastic augmentation $a_i$ produces
$\widetilde{x}_i=a_i(x_i)$, and the exact same tensor is supplied to teacher
and student. Alignment is necessary because a spatial target at position $p$
must refer to the same visible content in both models. Cross-entropy with label
smoothing remains the task anchor and trains every example, including examples
excluded from unreliable teacher imitation. In compact form, the step is
\[
\text{one sampled augmentation}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{identical teacher/student tensors}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}.
\]

\textbf{Intuition.} The spatial losses compare corresponding positions. If the
teacher sees one crop while the student sees another, position $p$ no longer
describes the same visible content and the target becomes contradictory.
Alignment is therefore a mathematical requirement for pointwise transfer, not
only an implementation convenience.

\paragraph{Step 2: global teacher-coordinate supervision (TCS).}
TCS transfers a partitioned version of the teacher's global representation and
is documented by the verified J1 specification \cite{chan2026j1}.
The teacher's globally pooled feature is projected through a frozen centred,
unwhitened PCA basis to $\bm z_i^{T,G}\in\mathbb{R}^{768}$. A training-only
student head predicts $\widehat{\bm z}_i^{S,G}\in\mathbb{R}^{768}$. A frozen
random-hyperplane bank has $\bm W_G\in\mathbb{R}^{768\times2048}$ and
$\bm b_G\in\mathbb{R}^{2048}$ and defines teacher bits
\begin{equation}
    \bm y_i^{T,G}
    =\mathbb{1}[\bm z_i^{T,G}\bm W_G+\bm b_G>0].
\end{equation}
A student predicts logits
$\bm h_i^{S,G}=\widehat{\bm z}_i^{S,G}\bm W_G+\bm b_G$ for the same
partitions. This partition-based transfer is inspired by random-hyperplane
locality-sensitive hashing, where sign agreement estimates angular similarity
without requiring coordinate identity \cite{charikar2002simhash}. Let
$m_i^T$ indicate that the teacher predicts the augmented image correctly and
$\ell_i^G$ be mean binary cross-entropy (BCE) across bits. Then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_G
    =\frac{\sum_i m_i^T\ell_i^G}
           {\max(\sum_i m_i^T,\varepsilon)}.
\label{eq:global-tcs}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Global TCS learns only from teacher-correct predictions.} If the
teacher predicts the augmented image incorrectly, then $m_i^T=0$ and that image
contributes no TCS loss. The same image still contributes to supervised CE, so
the ground-truth label can train the student without forcing it to imitate the
teacher's error. Global TCS remains active for the complete schedule. Its hard
partitions retain teacher geometry while allowing the student to move within a
half-space.

\textbf{Intuition.} CE says which class should win, but it does not describe
where the image lies inside the teacher's semantic space. TCS supplies many
binary half-space memberships. The student must reproduce those coarse
partitions but may move freely within a half-space, which is less restrictive
than exact coordinate regression. \textbf{The teacher-correct gate is therefore
a reliability filter: TCS transfers geometry only when the teacher also gets
the class decision right.}

\subsection{Early relational state}

At the E1 state, adaptive average pooling (AAP) first maps the feature tensor to
a fixed $q\times q$ grid. Each pooled spatial vector is then normalized, and
the resulting vectors are converted into spatial affinities. The complete step
is summarized below.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=5mm]
\node[lossstep] (features) {Teacher/student\\E1 feature maps};
\node[lossstep,right=of features] (pool) {AAP to fixed grids\\then normalize};
\node[lossstep,right=of pool] (affinity) {Global and local\\spatial affinities};
\node[lossstep,right=of affinity] (distance) {Centred-cosine distances\\equal branch mean};
\draw[lossarrow] (features) -- (pool);
\draw[lossarrow] (pool) -- (affinity);
\draw[lossarrow] (affinity) -- (distance);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

For pooled feature tensor $\bm X_i$, each spatial vector is normalized:
\begin{equation}
    \bm u_{i,p}
    =\frac{\bm x_{i,p}}{\|\bm x_{i,p}\|_2+\varepsilon},
    \qquad
    \bm G_i=\bm U_i\bm U_i^\top.
\end{equation}
The order is pooling, normalization, then affinity. Teacher and student channel
widths can differ because $G_i(p,r)$ compares spatial positions, not channels.

For descriptors $\bm s$ and $\bm t$, define centred cosine distance
\begin{equation}
    D(\bm s,\bm t)
    =1-\frac{\widetilde{\bm s}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm t}}
    {\|\widetilde{\bm s}\|_2\,
     \|\widetilde{\bm t}\|_2+\varepsilon},
\end{equation}
where each descriptor has its mean removed. The global E1 descriptor contains
all ordered off-diagonal affinities on a $7\times7$ grid. The local descriptor
contains directed eight-neighbour affinities on a $14\times14$ grid. Their
equal-weight mean is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{E1}
    =\tfrac12\mathcal{L}_{E1}^{\mathrm{global}}
     +\tfrac12\mathcal{L}_{E1}^{\mathrm{local}}.
\end{equation}
E1 directly updates only the E1-producing and earlier student modules. Later
features change through the next forward pass, but no loss forces the student
to copy the teacher's exact E1-to-M1 transformation.

\textbf{Why global and local?} The global branch uses every ordered
off-diagonal affinity on a $7\times7$ grid, so it captures long-range
coordination and whole-object topology at manageable quadratic cost. The local
branch retains a finer $14\times14$ grid but compares only directed
eight-neighbour pairs, preserving boundaries, parts, and short-range
continuity. Global relations alone can be too coarse for local structure,
whereas local relations alone cannot express coordination between distant
regions. Averaging the two branch losses gives these two questions equal
importance; it also prevents the much larger global pair set from dominating
solely through its number of entries. This use of spatial relations follows the
broader similarity-preserving and relational-distillation principle that a
student can reproduce geometry without matching teacher channels directly
\cite{park2019rkd,tung2019similarity}.

\textbf{Why AAP?} Teacher and student feature maps may differ in native size,
while affinity comparison requires corresponding positions. AAP creates fixed,
comparable grids, controls the quadratic cost of the global matrix, and reduces
high-frequency variation before relational comparison. Pooling is deliberately
performed \emph{before} vector normalization: the pooled vector then represents
the average feature content of a region. Reversing the order would first give
weak and noisy cells unit length and could let them distort the regional
average.

\textbf{Intuition.} E1 does not ask the student to reproduce teacher channel
values. It asks which early regions should be similar to one another, at both
whole-image and neighbourhood scales. This creates a stable relational anchor
while leaving the subsequent E1-to-M1 bridge available for a student-specific
solution.

\subsection{Late relational flow}

At $M1,M2,L1,L2$, construct global off-diagonal affinity descriptors
$\bm g_{i,\ell}$. For edge $a\rightarrow b$, the affinity change is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\bm g_{i,a\rightarrow b}
    =\bm g_{i,b}-\bm g_{i,a}.
\end{equation}
Global flow is matched over exactly $M1\rightarrow M2$,
$M2\rightarrow L1$, and $L1\rightarrow L2$. A local neighbour-flow term is
used only for $M1\rightarrow M2$, where both endpoints retain a compatible
$14\times14$ transition grid. The final loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}
    =\tfrac12\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{\mathrm{global}}
     +\tfrac12\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{\mathrm{local}}.
\end{equation}
This term transfers how spatial relations evolve, rather than only matching
their absolute states.

\textbf{Intuition.} State matching asks teacher and student to occupy the same
representation, which can be unnecessarily restrictive when their channel
widths and computational paths differ. Flow matching instead asks whether
spatial relations change in the same direction between selected states. The
student may therefore use a different absolute representation while preserving
the teacher's transition pattern, an idea related to inter-layer flow and
relational knowledge transfer \cite{yim2017fsp,park2019rkd}.

The local term is restricted to $M1\rightarrow M2$ because these endpoints
share the same $14\times14$ transition grid and therefore support position-wise
neighbour-change comparison. The global branch uses pooled descriptors and can
cross the later resolution changes. Combining the branches retains detailed
transition behaviour where the grids are compatible and long-range evolution
across the remainder of the hierarchy.

\subsection{Balanced PC1--32 transition coordinate}

The principal design difference from H9 is a unified variance-balanced
coordinate. At layer $\ell$, frozen teacher principal component analysis (PCA)
provides principal-component (PC) bands
\begin{align}
    \bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{T,(1)}
    &=(\bm f_{i,\ell,p}^T-\bm\mu_\ell)
      \bm V_{\ell,1:16},\\
    \bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{T,(2)}
    &=(\bm f_{i,\ell,p}^T-\bm\mu_\ell)
      \bm V_{\ell,17:32}.
\end{align}
Teacher-derived root-mean-square (RMS) scales $s_{\ell,1}$ and $s_{\ell,2}$ are fitted from a
500,000-coordinate internal-training reservoir and shared by teacher and
student. Separate training-only student heads predict the two 16-dimensional
bands. For either the teacher coordinates or corresponding student-head
outputs, the joint coordinate is
\begin{equation}
    \bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{32}
    =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
      \begin{bmatrix}
      \bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{(1)}/s_{\ell,1}\\
      \bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{(2)}/s_{\ell,2}
      \end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:j32-coordinate}
\end{equation}
Balancing prevents the high-variance PC1--16 band from overwhelming PC17--32;
the separate student heads remain, but one dense bank and one loss optimize the
joint 32-dimensional geometry.

A frozen dense hyperplane bank has
$\bm W_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times M_\ell}$, where
$M_{M1}=M_{M2}=256$ and $M_{L1}=M_{L2}=512$. Its bias is fitted from the
teacher reservoir as
\begin{equation}
 b_{\ell,j}=-\operatorname*{median}_{n}
 \left[(\bm z_{n,\ell}^{T,32})^\top\bm w_{\ell,j}\right].
\end{equation}
Teacher targets use the verified inclusive convention
$y_{i,\ell,p,j}^{T}=\mathbb{1}[a_{i,\ell,p,j}^{T}\geq0]$, whereas student
head outputs provide logits $h_{i,\ell,p,j}^{S}$. The per-position mismatch is
\begin{equation}
\ell_{i,\ell,p}
=\frac{1}{M_\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{M_\ell}
\left[-y_{i,\ell,p,j}^{T}\log\sigma(h_{i,\ell,p,j}^{S})
-(1-y_{i,\ell,p,j}^{T})\log(1-\sigma(h_{i,\ell,p,j}^{S}))\right].
\label{eq:j32-bit-loss}
\end{equation}
Spatial weights are computed separately in the two bands:
\begin{equation}
    w_{i,\ell,p}
    =\frac12\left[
      \frac{\|\bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{T,(1)}\|_2+\varepsilon}
           {\sum_q(\|\bm z_{i,\ell,q}^{T,(1)}\|_2+\varepsilon)}
      +
      \frac{\|\bm z_{i,\ell,p}^{T,(2)}\|_2+\varepsilon}
           {\sum_q(\|\bm z_{i,\ell,q}^{T,(2)}\|_2+\varepsilon)}
    \right].
\label{eq:j32-weights}
\end{equation}
Each band receives half the spatial importance budget. The per-image loss is
$L_{i,\ell}^{J32}=\sum_p w_{i,\ell,p}\ell_{i,\ell,p}$, and teacher-correct
gating is applied only after bit and spatial reduction:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{J32}^{(\ell)}
    =\frac{\sum_i m_i^T L_{i,\ell}^{J32}}
           {\max(\sum_i m_i^T,1)}.
\label{eq:j32-gate}
\end{equation}
There is no segmentation mask or additional background threshold.

\textbf{Intuition.} The two PCA bands retain leading and lower-variance
teacher directions, while RMS balancing prevents PC1--16 from numerically
overwhelming PC17--32. Dense random hyperplanes turn this coordinate into many
binary geometric questions: the student must enter the same partitions as the
teacher but need not reproduce exact coordinate magnitudes
\cite{charikar2002simhash}. Teacher energy weights emphasize spatial positions
that carry stronger signal in either band, and the teacher-correct gate is
applied only after the spatial and bit reductions so that it rejects an
unreliable image-level target without changing the within-image weighting.

\subsection{Gradient calibration and terminal release}

Raw loss coefficients are not comparable because the terms have different
scales and parameter paths. Each $J32$ layer coefficient $\gamma_\ell$ is
fitted to a target backbone-gradient ratio relative to
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+6\mathcal{L}_G$. This design is informed by
multi-objective and adaptive loss-balancing work, but freezes the calibrated
ratios rather than learning them online
\cite{chen2018gradnorm,kendall2018multitask,sener2018multiobjective,
yu2020pcgrad}. Targets for
$M1,M2,L1,L2$ are $(0.050,0.040,0.025,0.015)$. E1 and flow are jointly
Gram-calibrated so their combined gradient norm is $0.08$ of the calibrated
base. The frozen coefficients were approximately
$(\gamma_{M1},\gamma_{M2},\gamma_{L1},\gamma_{L2})
=(19.2733,77.5578,19.9431,17.1116)$,
$\lambda_E=9.7376$, and $\lambda_F=0.0028157$. Their scalar magnitudes should
be interpreted through the achieved gradient ratios, not compared directly.
This controls training pressure, making ablations more informative about the
transferred object rather than accidental scalar magnitude.

One shared multiplier controls J32, E1, and flow:
\begin{equation}
m(e)=
\begin{cases}
1, & 0\leq e\leq100,\\
(116-e)/16, & 100<e<116,\\
0, & 116\leq e\leq119.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:j1-schedule}
\end{equation}
The final four epochs retain CE and global TCS only, allowing the student to
consolidate without active spatial constraints. All auxiliary heads and fitted
assets are removed at deployment.

\textbf{Intuition.} A coefficient of $20$ does not imply that one knowledge
object is twenty times more important than another: the underlying losses may
produce very different gradients. Calibration instead controls their actual
pressure on the shared backbone, making comparisons more faithful to the
transferred object. The shared decay then gradually removes the spatial
constraints, and the terminal release leaves CE and global TCS to consolidate
the final classifier and image-level geometry before all training-only assets
are discarded.
