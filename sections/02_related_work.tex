\section{Critical Literature Review}\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Explanation, attribution, and representation analysis}

Interpretability terminology is used inconsistently. In this report, a
\emph{representation diagnostic} describes structure inside a network without
necessarily assigning causal credit for one prediction. An
\emph{attribution method} assigns importance to input regions or internal
features for a selected output. A \emph{visual explanation} is the
human-facing result of such an analysis together with a stated interpretation.
These distinctions matter because a visually plausible object map may describe
objectness without identifying the evidence that caused a particular class
score.

Early network-visualization work reconstructed or optimized image patterns that
activate internal units, revealing a progression from low-level features to
higher-level parts \cite{zeiler2014visualizing,yosinski2015deepvis}.
These studies support layer-wise inspection, but they do not provide a simple
spatial explanation for an individual decision. Attribution methods instead
begin with a prediction and distribute relevance over the input or an internal
map. Examples include layer-wise relevance propagation
\cite{bach2015pixelwise}, integrated gradients
\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, perturbation methods such as RISE
\cite{petsiuk2018rise}, and the CAM family reviewed below.

\subsection{Class activation mapping}

Original CAM uses global-average-pooled convolutional channels and learned
classifier weights to produce a class-specific spatial map
\cite{zhou2016cam}. Its architectural constraint motivated Grad-CAM, which
replaces classifier weights with spatially averaged gradients of a target logit
with respect to an activation channel \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}. If
$A^k_{ij}$ is activation channel $k$ and $Y^c$ is the target logit, Grad-CAM
uses
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_k^c=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j}
    \frac{\partial Y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}},\qquad
    M^c_{\mathrm{GC}}=\operatorname{ReLU}
    \left(\sum_k\alpha_k^c A^k\right).
\end{equation}
Grad-CAM++ introduces spatially varying higher-order gradient weights to better
handle multiple occurrences and localization
\cite{chattopadhay2018gradcampp}. LayerCAM retains positive gradients locally
instead of reducing them to one channel coefficient, enabling hierarchical
maps from multiple layers \cite{jiang2021layercam}.

Gradient-free variants address gradient saturation or instability. Score-CAM
uses the change in class score produced by activation-guided masks
\cite{wang2020scorecam}, while Ablation-CAM estimates channel importance
through channel ablation \cite{desai2020ablationcam}. EigenCAM is
class-agnostic: it extracts the dominant principal direction of an activation
tensor rather than differentiating a selected output
\cite{muhammad2020eigencam}. These methods do not answer exactly the same
question. Class-conditioned maps prioritize evidence for a selected class;
EigenCAM and \methodname{} describe activation or feature geometry without
requiring a target.

\subsection{Limitations and evaluation of visual explanations}

CAM variants expose several recurring trade-offs. Late layers are semantically
mature but spatially coarse. Earlier layers retain boundaries and local detail
but may not yet be class-discriminative. Upsampling a $7\times7$ map cannot
recover detail that is absent in the tapped representation. Global gradient
aggregation may suppress spatially heterogeneous evidence, whereas local
weighting can be noisy. Gradient-free perturbation can improve robustness at a
higher computational cost. Most importantly, a map can look plausible without
being faithful to the model.

Sanity-check research has shown that some saliency methods can remain visually
similar after model parameters or labels are randomized
\cite{adebayo2018sanity}. Consequently, evaluation should combine several
properties:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Localization:} overlap with object masks or pointing-game
    accuracy, ideally under an exact-area protocol;
    \item \textbf{Faithfulness:} prediction change under deletion, insertion,
    or other controlled perturbations;
    \item \textbf{Class sensitivity:} change when the target class changes,
    when class attribution is claimed;
    \item \textbf{Stability:} consistency under small input perturbations and
    repeated training;
    \item \textbf{Sanity:} degradation after parameter or label
    randomization; and
    \item \textbf{Human utility:} whether the explanation helps a user perform
    a defined task rather than merely appearing intuitive.
\end{itemize}

No single metric is sufficient. Fixed-area intersection-over-union measures
object coverage but not classifier causality. Deleting highly ranked pixels is
closer to a causal intervention, but the result depends on the perturbation and
may reward concentrated class evidence over complete object extent. This
report therefore treats localization and causality as separate axes.

\subsection{Representations across depth}

Representational analysis asks how information is organized rather than which
pixels caused one output. Linear probes test whether labels can be decoded from
intermediate states \cite{alain2017linearprobes}. Canonical correlation and
related methods compare representation subspaces
\cite{raghu2017svcca,kornblith2019cka}. PCA identifies orthogonal directions of
maximum dataset variance and can reduce a high-dimensional feature vector to a
small number of coordinates for visualization. Because PCA axes are defined up
to sign and can rotate when eigenvalues are close, meaningful comparisons
require a frozen basis and explicit alignment.

Late classifiers may also exhibit neural-collapse-like organization:
within-class variability contracts, class means become more separated and
approach a simplex equiangular geometry, and nearest-class-center prediction
becomes accurate \cite{papyan2020neuralcollapse}. This provides a useful model
of class-semantic maturity, but it does not imply that every useful spatial
prototype is a class mean. The \methodname{} experiments specifically find a
small number of broad spatial modes whose class purity is low; these modes are
therefore distinct from the full class simplex.

The open gap is not whether early and late layers differ; that is well
established. The gap addressed here is whether a reproducible intermediate
transition can be identified jointly through spatial structure, semantic
maturity, and geometric change, and whether that transition supplies a useful
explanatory or transferable object.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation}

Knowledge distillation (KD) trains a compact student using information from a
larger teacher. Classical KD transfers softened output distributions
\cite{hinton2015distilling}. FitNets introduced intermediate hints
\cite{romero2015fitnets}, and attention transfer matches spatial summaries of
feature energy \cite{zagoruyko2017attention}. FSP matrices transfer relationships
between successive feature stages \cite{yim2017fsp}, while Overhaul Feature
Distillation uses selective feature imitation and margin-based handling
\cite{heo2019ofd}.

More recent methods address layer association and hierarchical transfer.
SemCKD learns semantic calibration between possible teacher and student layers
\cite{chen2021semckd}. ReviewKD uses a review path that combines multi-level
student features and compares them with teacher stages
\cite{chen2021reviewkd}. MGD masks student features and trains a generator to
reconstruct teacher representations \cite{yang2022mgd}. These methods establish
strong baselines, but they also expose a central design tension: direct matching
may transfer useful structure, yet forcing a smaller student to reproduce every
teacher state or transition can create negative regularization.

The \jmethod{} direction is selective. It does not claim that more supervised
layers are always better. It transfers different invariants in different
semantic zones: an early channel-invariant relational state, balanced
teacher-coordinate partitions at selected transition and late layers, and the
change in affinities only after semantic maturity. The E1-to-M1 bridge is left
student-specific.

\subsection{Vision transformers and future scope}

Vision Transformers (ViTs) replace convolutional grids with patch tokens and
self-attention \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}; DeiT improves data-efficient
supervised training \cite{touvron2021deit}, while Swin introduces a
hierarchical shifted-window design \cite{liu2021swin}. Attention weights are
visually tempting but do not automatically measure feature importance.
Attention rollout composes attention across layers
\cite{abnar2020attentionflow}, and relevance-propagation methods account for
attention, residual connections, and class-specific gradients
\cite{chefer2021transformer}. Current work continues to address gradient flow
and attribution quality in ViTs \cite{mehri2025libragrad}.

This literature makes a fixed transformer plan premature. A flat ViT, a
hierarchical Swin model, and a self-supervised foundation model may exhibit
different transition objects. The future study will therefore begin by asking
whether spatial/token geometry shows a measurable transition, rather than
assuming that CNN layers, PCA colour, or \methodname{} can be transferred
unchanged.

\subsection{Synthesis of research gaps}

Among the methods reviewed here, three areas remain underexplored:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item output-to-space attribution is much more developed than direct
    characterization of spatial representation formation across depth;
    \item class-agnostic, label-free spatial geometry remains underexplored as
    an explanation principle whose localization and classifier faithfulness are
    evaluated separately; and
    \item explicit representation-transition hypotheses are not yet a common
    basis for deciding where knowledge should be preserved or released in the
    distillation methods reviewed.
\end{enumerate}
