\section{Progress I: Representation-Transition Discovery}\label{sec:pca-progress}

\textbf{Progress I identifies a reproducible late representation transition
and distinguishes it from the gradual accumulation of decodable class
information.} The completed study uses train-fitted, layer-specific principal
component analysis (PCA), calibration-only transition selection, held-out
evaluation, architecture-matched random controls, label-permutation nulls, and
distance-sensitivity tests. The evidence follows a fixed direction: fit the
representation instrument on training data, select the candidate transition on
calibration data, and use the evaluation split only for the reported figures
and tables.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsection{Frozen protocol and PCA-RGB construction}

For layer $\ell$, model condition $c$, image $x_i$, and spatial position
$p$, let
\begin{equation}
    \bm f^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_\ell}
\end{equation}
be the channel vector at that position. The condition index distinguishes the
trained network from each architecture-matched random initialization. It is
included here because trained and random networks use separately fitted PCA
bases.

Let $\mathcal T_{\ell,c}$ contain the spatial vectors sampled across the
fit-training images, and let $N_{\ell,c}=|\mathcal T_{\ell,c}|$. The training
mean and covariance are
\begin{align}
    \bm\mu_{\ell,c}
    &=
    \frac{1}{N_{\ell,c}}
    \sum_{(j,q)\in\mathcal T_{\ell,c}}
    \bm f^{(\ell,c)}_{j,q},
    \label{eq:pca-training-mean}\\
    \bm\Sigma_{\ell,c}
    &=
    \frac{1}{N_{\ell,c}-1}
    \sum_{(j,q)\in\mathcal T_{\ell,c}}
    \bigl(\bm f^{(\ell,c)}_{j,q}-\bm\mu_{\ell,c}\bigr)
    \bigl(\bm f^{(\ell,c)}_{j,q}-\bm\mu_{\ell,c}\bigr)^\top .
    \label{eq:pca-training-covariance}
\end{align}
These quantities are accumulated with streaming statistics, so the complete
feature population does not have to be stored in memory. Principal component
analysis then solves
\begin{equation}
    \bm\Sigma_{\ell,c}\bm v_{\ell,c,k}
    =
    \lambda_{\ell,c,k}\bm v_{\ell,c,k},
    \qquad
    \lambda_{\ell,c,1}\geq\lambda_{\ell,c,2}\geq\cdots ,
    \label{eq:pca-eigendecomposition}
\end{equation}
where the orthonormal eigenvectors give the directions of decreasing training
variance \cite{pearson1901pca,hotelling1933pca}.

An eigenvector and its negative describe the same PCA axis, but they would
reverse the corresponding display colour. To make reruns deterministic, the
largest-magnitude loading is required to be positive. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
    a_{\ell,c,k}
    =
    \underset{d}{\arg\max}\,
    \left|[\bm v_{\ell,c,k}]_d\right|,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\bm v}_{\ell,c,k}
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \bm v_{\ell,c,k},
        & [\bm v_{\ell,c,k}]_{a_{\ell,c,k}}\geq 0,\\
        -\bm v_{\ell,c,k},
        & [\bm v_{\ell,c,k}]_{a_{\ell,c,k}}<0.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:pca-sign-fixing}
\end{equation}

For a new image, each spatial feature vector is centred with the frozen
training mean and projected onto the first three signed components:
\begin{equation}
    z^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,k}
    =
    \widetilde{\bm v}_{\ell,c,k}^{\top}
    \bigl(\bm f^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p}-\bm\mu_{\ell,c}\bigr),
    \qquad k\in\{1,2,3\}.
    \label{eq:pca-coordinate}
\end{equation}
The three coordinates can have very different numerical ranges. A fixed,
symmetric display scale is therefore estimated for each component from the
absolute fit-training scores:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\ell,c,k}
    =
    Q_{0.995}
    \left(
        \left\{
        \left|z^{(\ell,c)}_{j,q,k}\right|
        :(j,q)\in\mathcal T_{\ell,c}
        \right\}
    \right),
    \label{eq:pca-display-scale}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{0.995}$ denotes the $99.5$th percentile. The displayed channel value
at position $p$ is
\begin{equation}
    C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,k}
    =
    \operatorname{clip}
    \left(
        \frac{1}{2}
        +
        \frac{z^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,k}}
             {2\max(s_{\ell,c,k},\varepsilon)},
        0,1
    \right),
    \qquad
    \varepsilon=10^{-12}.
    \label{eq:pca-rgb-channel}
\end{equation}
The final colour at that position is
\begin{equation}
    \bm C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p}
    =
    \left(
        C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,1},
        C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,2},
        C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p,3}
    \right)
    =
    (R_{i,p},G_{i,p},B_{i,p}).
    \label{eq:pca-rgb}
\end{equation}
A zero PCA score maps to the neutral value $0.5$. Scores of
$-s_{\ell,c,k}$ and $+s_{\ell,c,k}$ map to $0$ and $1$, respectively, and more
extreme values are clipped. Thus, the first component controls red, the second
controls green, and the third controls blue.

For one image, the RGB vectors from all $H_\ell W_\ell$ spatial positions are
returned to their feature-grid locations and resized only for display:
\begin{equation}
    \bm M_i^{(\ell,c)}
    =
    \operatorname{Resize}
    \left(
        \operatorname{reshape}_{H_\ell\times W_\ell}
        \left(
            \left[\bm C^{(\ell,c)}_{i,p}\right]_
            {p=1}^{H_\ell W_\ell}
        \right)
    \right).
    \label{eq:pca-rgb-image}
\end{equation}
The resize is bilinear and does not change the PCA coordinates or any reported
metric. \textbf{No per-image minimum--maximum normalization is applied.} The mean,
basis, component signs, and display scales are fitted once from fit-training
vectors and then reused for every calibration and evaluation image.

\textbf{PCA alignment is dataset-wide within a layer, but it is not shared
across layers.} Every image at layer $\ell$ uses the same basis, so same-layer
colour and coordinate comparisons are meaningful. Each layer nevertheless has
its own basis. Therefore, a red region at one layer is not assumed to represent
the same direction at another layer. Cross-depth conclusions are based on
prototype agreement, true-class margin, between/within separation, effective
rank, centred kernel alignment (CKA), and frozen transition scores rather than
on colour identity alone.

The experimental protocol separates training, calibration, and evaluation
roles. PCA bases and class prototypes are fitted from training data. The
transition selector uses calibration data only. Evaluation images and labels
are not used to fit or rank candidate layers. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 use trained
ConvNeXt checkpoints and three architecture-matched random initializations.
ImageNet-1K uses a pretrained ConvNeXt checkpoint for visualization and
representation measurement; no ImageNet-1K model was trained as part of this
study.

\textbf{The trained checkpoints have strong classification accuracy, which is
reported separately from the prototype accuracy studied below.} CIFAR-10 uses
the ConvNeXt-Small checkpoint
\path{clr4takeoff/convnext-small-cifar10} \cite{clr4takeoff2026convnextcifar10}.
Under the frozen preprocessing protocol, it correctly classifies 9,775 of the
10,000 official test images, giving \textbf{97.75\% Top-1 accuracy}. CIFAR-100
uses the ConvNeXt-Base checkpoint
\path{anonauthors/cifar100-ConvNeXt-base}
\cite{anonauthors2026convnextcifar100}. It correctly classifies 9,057 of the
10,000 official test images, giving \textbf{90.57\% Top-1 accuracy}. These two
figures were measured locally using the exact frozen checkpoints and
preprocessing used in Progress I. ImageNet-1K uses the pretrained
\path{timm/convnext_small.fb_in1k} checkpoint
\cite{wightman2026convnextsmallfbin1k}. Its official model comparison reports
83.142\% Top-1 and 96.434\% Top-5 accuracy at 224-pixel resolution. These
ImageNet-1K values are reference results rather than measurements from this
study: the checkpoint was used without additional training on a frozen
5,000-image cohort for visualization and representation analysis, and its
full-validation classification accuracy was not independently re-evaluated.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsection{Visual progression across depth}

The complete depth atlases show a recurring descriptive sequence:
\begin{equation}
    \text{feature preservation}
    \longrightarrow
    \text{representation transition}
    \longrightarrow
    \text{class-oriented geometry}.
    \label{eq:pca-progression}
\end{equation}
Early and intermediate maps retain edges, shapes, and spatial layouts. Near the
late transition, the maps reorganize abruptly. Late CIFAR-10 maps from the same
class become visually similar in a shared layer basis, whereas the same visual
regularity is weaker for CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1K. This difference is expected:
three displayed coordinates cannot represent all class structure when class
count and intra-class diversity increase.

\textbf{The important evidence is therefore not the visual colour change by
itself, but the agreement between that change and basis-invariant metrics.}
The following analyses test whether the visible transition reflects learned
class geometry, where it occurs, and whether it survives changes in dataset,
distance, and selector composition.

    \label{fig:pca-cifar10-random-control}
\end{figure}

\input{tables/pca_rgb/PCA-TAB-03_trained_vs_random.tex}

\Cref{fig:pca-cifar10-random-control,tab:pca-trained-random-main} separate
architecture-induced visual structure from learned semantic organization.
Randomly initialized networks retain visible patterns and achieve prototype
accuracy above a pure label-permutation null. Their mean prototype accuracy is
$0.213$ on CIFAR-10 and approximately $0.039$ on CIFAR-100. This is important:
convolution, residual connections, input statistics, and spatial pooling can
produce label-correlated structure even without supervised learning.

Here, \textbf{prototype accuracy is the proportion of held-out images whose
pooled representation is assigned to the correct class by the nearest
fit-training class prototype}. It tests whether samples are organized around
simple class centres rather than whether a new classifier can learn to decode
them. The prototypes and the accuracy calculation are defined formally in
\cref{subsec:pca-transition-selection}.

The backbone weights in each random control remain fixed. Class prototypes are
estimated from its fit-training descriptors and then evaluated on held-out
images. When a linear probe is reported later, the protocol trains only that
linear classifier on the first globally averaged representation from the
frozen feature map. It does not train or fine-tune the random backbone. This
distinction makes the control interpretable: the experiment measures what the
architecture already exposes and compares it with the additional organization
created by supervised feature learning.

\textbf{The evidence for learned semantics is the large trained--random gap and
its abrupt late-depth growth, not the mere presence of a visible pattern.} At
L36, trained prototype accuracy reaches $0.969$ on CIFAR-10 and $0.864$ on
CIFAR-100, compared with random means of $0.213$ and $0.039$. The resulting
trained--random differences are $0.756$ and $0.824$. The random controls do not
show the corresponding late transition.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{Selecting the transition without evaluation leakage}
\label{subsec:pca-transition-selection}

The visible PCA-RGB progression suggests where to look, but the transition is
selected with numerical measures that do not depend on colour interpretation.
Let the spatially pooled descriptor for image $i$ at layer $\ell$ be
$\bm r_i^{(\ell)}$. Let
$\mathcal T_k=\{i\in\mathcal T:y_i=k\}$ denote the indices of fit-training
images whose label is class $k$. Thus, $|\mathcal T_k|$ is the number of
fit-training images in that class. After unit normalization, the class-$k$
prototype is
\begin{equation}
    \bm c_k^{(\ell)}
    =
    \operatorname{norm}
    \left(
        \frac{1}{|\mathcal T_k|}
        \sum_{i\in\mathcal T_k}
        \operatorname{norm}(\bm r_i^{(\ell)})
    \right),
    \qquad
    \operatorname{norm}(\bm a)=\frac{\bm a}{\max(\|\bm a\|_2,\varepsilon)}.
    \label{eq:pca-class-prototype}
\end{equation}
The prototype prediction chooses the class with the largest cosine similarity,
$\hat y_i^{(\ell)}=\arg\max_k\langle
\operatorname{norm}(\bm r_i^{(\ell)}),\bm c_k^{(\ell)}\rangle$.
\textbf{Prototype accuracy is the fraction of images for which this nearest
prototype label is correct.} It measures whether class membership is expressed
as a simple class-centre geometry, rather than whether an independently trained
classifier can decode the representation.

The true-class margin is
\begin{equation}
    m_i^{(\ell)}
    =
    \left\langle\operatorname{norm}(\bm r_i^{(\ell)}),
    \bm c_{y_i}^{(\ell)}\right\rangle
    -
    \max_{k\ne y_i}
    \left\langle\operatorname{norm}(\bm r_i^{(\ell)}),
    \bm c_k^{(\ell)}\right\rangle .
    \label{eq:pca-true-class-margin}
\end{equation}
A positive value means the correct prototype is closer than every competing
prototype. The report plots the mean margin, so a sharp rise indicates that the
correct class centre has become more clearly preferred.

Between-class to within-class separation compares the spread of class centres
with the residual spread of samples around their correct centre. With
$\bar{\bm c}=K^{-1}\sum_k\bm c_k$, it is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{B_\ell}{W_\ell}
    =
    \frac{
        K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\bm c_k^{(\ell)}-\bar{\bm c}^{(\ell)}\|_2^2
    }{
        N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
        \|\operatorname{norm}(\bm r_i^{(\ell)})-\bm c_{y_i}^{(\ell)}\|_2^2
        +\varepsilon
    }.
    \label{eq:pca-bw-separation}
\end{equation}
Larger values indicate more separation between classes relative to the
variation within each class. The figure uses $\log(B_\ell/W_\ell)$ because the
ratio spans a wide range. The logarithm makes proportional changes readable;
zero means $B_\ell=W_\ell$, while a positive value means between-class spread
is larger.

Centred kernel alignment (CKA) compares the representation geometry of the
same calibration images at two adjacent layers \cite{kornblith2019cka}.
For column-centred descriptor matrices $\bm X$ and $\bm Y$, linear CKA is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{CKA}(\bm X,\bm Y)
    =
    \frac{\|\bm X^{\top}\bm Y\|_F^2}
    {\|\bm X^{\top}\bm X\|_F\,\|\bm Y^{\top}\bm Y\|_F}.
    \label{eq:pca-linear-cka}
\end{equation}
CKA is near one when the two layers preserve similar pairwise geometry.
\textbf{The report plots $1-\mathrm{CKA}$ so that a larger value consistently
means a larger adjacent representation change.} This quantity is called the
adjacent change in the figure.

The selector combines the three complementary signals. For every eligible
calibration tap, define
\begin{equation}
    \Delta A_\ell=A_\ell-A_{\ell-1},\qquad
    \Delta G_\ell=\log(B_\ell/W_\ell)-\log(B_{\ell-1}/W_{\ell-1}),
    \qquad C_\ell=1-\operatorname{CKA}_{\ell-1,\ell}.
\end{equation}
Each component is converted to a $z$-score across eligible taps. The full
selector is
\begin{equation}
    S_\ell
    =\frac{1}{3}
    \left[z(\Delta A_\ell)+z(\Delta G_\ell)+z(C_\ell)\right].
    \label{eq:pca-transition-selector}
\end{equation}
The highest-scoring non-terminal calibration tap is selected. The score is a
dimensionless ranking value, not an accuracy. Its equal weights ask for a layer
that simultaneously improves prototype agreement, increases class separation,
and changes adjacent geometry. The component audit below shows how the choice
changes when one signal is removed or when the trained score is compared with
the mean random-network score.

    \centering
    \pcaresultfigure[width=\linewidth]{pca_rgb/PCA-FIG-04_cifar10_transition_metrics_compact.pdf}
    \caption{CIFAR-10 transition metrics across normalized depth. The selected
    L36 transition is marked by the vertical dashed line. The grey reference is
    the architecture-matched random mean with one standard deviation.}
    \label{fig:pca-cifar10-transition}
\end{figure}

The selector combines three calibration-only signals: increasing prototype

\textbf{The selected layer is not an artefact of one selector component.}
Removing CKA, prototype accuracy, or separation individually still selects L36
on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Semantic-only and trained-specific selector
variants also select L36. This agreement supports the location of the
transition, although it does not imply that every selector term is equally
necessary in other architectures.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{The transition reorganizes existing class information}

The focused analysis places additional taps immediately before and after the
late downsampling-layer normalization (LN), followed by the downsampling
convolution and the next block. This lets the experiment ask whether class
information first appears at the transition or whether the transition changes
the geometry of information that was already accessible.

In the first column of \cref{fig:pca-transition-mechanism},
\textbf{PCA-$k$ cosine-prototype accuracy} means that fit-training features are
projected onto their first $k$ principal components, pooled, normalized, and
classified by the nearest cosine class prototype. The curve shows whether a
small or larger PCA subspace is needed to recover class-centre organization.
\textbf{Raw cosine-prototype accuracy} in the second column applies the same
nearest-prototype rule to the complete, unprojected pooled feature. The linear
probe in that column is different: it is an affine classifier trained on the
fit-training pooled features while every backbone parameter remains frozen.
High probe accuracy with low prototype accuracy means that class information is
decodable but is not yet arranged around simple cosine class centres.

The third column reports effective rank. If $p_j=\lambda_j/\sum_q\lambda_q$
is the normalized PCA spectrum, then
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{eff}}
    =\exp\left(-\sum_j p_j\log p_j\right).
    \label{eq:pca-effective-rank}
\end{equation}
An effective rank near one means that almost all variation lies along one
direction; a larger value means that variation is distributed across more
directions. The final column audits the selector defined in
\cref{eq:pca-transition-selector}. ``Full'' uses all three equally weighted
components. ``No CKA'', ``no prototype'', and ``no separation'' remove one
component. ``Semantic'' keeps only non-negative prototype improvement and log
separation. ``Trained'' subtracts the random-seed mean selector score from the
trained score. Their values are ranking scores, not percentages.

\input{tables/pca_rgb/PCA-TAB-06_focused_transition_controls.tex}

The focused controls distinguish the emergence of linearly decodable class
information from a change in how that information is geometrically organized.
At Pre-LN, linear-probe Top-1 is already $0.976$ on CIFAR-10 and $0.911$ on
CIFAR-100. Across Pre-LN to Post-LN, it rises by only $0.0032$ and $0.0046$.
The transition therefore cannot be described as the first appearance of class
information.

\textbf{Across the same operation, PCA-128 cosine-prototype accuracy rises by
$0.5948$ on CIFAR-10 and $0.5719$ on CIFAR-100.} Effective rank changes from
$1.65$ to $50.79$ on CIFAR-10 and from $1.47$ to $88.53$ on CIFAR-100. The
large incoming one-minus-CKA value at Post-LN further indicates a marked change
in representation geometry. Together, these results support a more precise
interpretation: \textbf{Layer normalization at the focused late transition
reconditions already decodable class information into a higher-rank,
prototype-aligned geometry.}

This conclusion is localized to the tested ConvNeXt transition and should not
be generalized into a claim that every normalization layer performs the same
role. The broader contribution is the distinction between information being
decodable and information being organized into a compact, prototype-aligned
geometry.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsection{Robustness to PCA dimension and distance}

\input{tables/pca_rgb/PCA-TAB-08_pca_dimensionality_distance_sensitivity.tex}

The Pre-LN result is sensitive to representation and distance. For CIFAR-10,
Pre-LN cosine-prototype accuracy is $0.855$ in the raw representation but
$0.375$ after PCA-128; for CIFAR-100 it changes from $0.508$ to $0.332$.
RMS-Euclidean accuracy is substantially higher at the same Pre-LN state. This
shows that the highly anisotropic, very low effective-rank representation can
look different under different metrics.

\textbf{Post-LN class organization is substantially more stable.} CIFAR-10
Post-LN prototype accuracy is approximately $0.970$ under cosine, Euclidean,
and RMS-Euclidean comparisons. CIFAR-100 remains approximately $0.90$ across
the same choices. The transition is therefore not explained by a favourable
choice of only one distance function or by displaying only the first three
principal components.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{CIFAR-100 hierarchy and class prototypes}

The fourth panel of \cref{fig:pca-cifar100-hierarchy} reports the
\textbf{explained variance ratio (EVR)}, which measures how much of the total
feature variance is carried by a principal component. If
$\lambda_{\ell,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell,D_\ell}$ are the covariance
eigenvalues at layer $\ell$, the first-component EVR is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{EVR}_{1}^{(\ell)}
    =
    \frac{\lambda_{\ell,1}}
         {\sum_{j=1}^{D_\ell}\lambda_{\ell,j}}.
    \label{eq:pca-evr-one}
\end{equation}
The figure plots $\operatorname{EVR}_{1}^{(\ell)}\times D_\ell$ so that $1$
has an intuitive reference meaning: every dimension would carry an equal
share of variance. A value above $1$ shows how many times more variance the
first principal component carries than that equal-share baseline. A large
value therefore indicates a strongly dominant direction, whereas effective
rank summarizes how broadly variance is distributed over the full spectrum.

their broader hierarchy, but the late representation increasingly prioritizes
the fine classification objective.}

\subsection{Incorrect predictions follow learned semantic geometry}

For an incorrectly classified evaluation example with true class $y_i$ and
predicted class $\hat y_i$, define
\begin{equation}
    \Delta d_i^{(\ell)}
    = d(\bm r_i^{(\ell)},\bm c_{y_i}^{(\ell)})
      -d(\bm r_i^{(\ell)},\bm c_{\hat y_i}^{(\ell)}),
    \label{eq:pca-error-margin}
\end{equation}
where $\bm r_i^{(\ell)}$ is the pooled representation and
$\bm c_k^{(\ell)}$ is the fit-training class prototype. Positive $\Delta d$
means that the sample is closer to the predicted-class prototype than to the
true-class prototype.

    \centering
    \pcaresultfigure[width=\linewidth]{pca_rgb/PCA-FIG-08_cifar100_incorrect_prediction_alignment_compact.pdf}
    \caption{Frozen CIFAR-100 incorrect-prediction cases at L39. Positive
    $\Delta d$ indicates greater proximity to the predicted-class prototype.
    Both supporting cases and counterexamples are retained. Alignment is
    associative and should not be interpreted as causal attribution. Additional
    cases are retained in
    \hyperref[fig:pca-cifar100-errors-appendix]{Appendix Figure~\ref*{fig:pca-cifar100-errors-appendix}}.}
    \label{fig:pca-cifar100-errors}
\end{figure}

\input{tables/pca_rgb/PCA-TAB-05_wrong_prediction_alignment.tex}

On CIFAR-10, the fraction of errors closer to the predicted prototype rises
from $0.507$ at L35 to $0.778$ at L36 and $0.933$ at L39. On CIFAR-100, the
corresponding fractions are $0.555$, $0.662$, and $0.749$. At L39, coarse-class
errors show stronger alignment ($0.809$) than fine-class errors that preserve
the correct coarse group ($0.706$).

\textbf{Late geometry therefore often agrees with the model's mistaken
semantic decision, but not in every case.} The negative examples in
\cref{fig:pca-cifar100-errors} are scientifically important. They prevent the
result from being interpreted as a deterministic rule. Prototype proximity is
an association within the learned representation; it does not establish which
pixels caused the classifier's output.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsection{Label-permutation null}

In this analysis, a \textbf{null} is the reference result expected when class
labels have no systematic relationship with the learned representations. The
feature vectors and held-out evaluation labels are kept fixed. The
fit-training labels are randomly permuted 100 times, preserving the number of
examples assigned to each class. Class prototypes are recomputed after every
permutation and evaluated against the unchanged true evaluation labels. This
breaks the training-label--feature association while retaining the model,
features, sample sizes, and class balance.

The term \textbf{late permutation null} means that this same control is applied
at a late tap such as L36 or L39; it is not a separate network or an additional
training run. The \textbf{null mean} is the average accuracy across the 100
permutations. The \textbf{null 95\% interval} is the empirical range from the
2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of those accuracies. An observed accuracy above
the interval's upper limit is therefore much larger than expected from random
label assignment under this control.

\input{tables/pca_rgb/PCA-TAB-09_label_permutation_null.tex}

The fit-training label-permutation analysis confirms two complementary facts.
First, trained L36 and L39 prototype accuracies lie far outside their null
intervals. Second, random networks also exceed their permutation nulls:
approximately $0.213$ versus a $0.10$ null mean on CIFAR-10, and $0.039$ versus
a $0.01$ null mean on CIFAR-100. \textbf{Random architecture can preserve weak
dataset-correlated organization, while supervised training produces the much
larger and sharply localized semantic transition.} This distinction replaces
the overly simple claim that all visible random-network structure is
meaningless.

The calibration-only selector identifies L36, at normalized depth $0.923$, for

$0.969$ for CIFAR-10, $0.864$ for CIFAR-100, and $0.594$ for ImageNet-1K.

\textbf{The cross-dataset evidence supports the transition mechanism more
strongly than it supports visually uniform late class colours.} The late
change in prototype accuracy, separation, CKA, and transition score remains
visible as dataset complexity increases. However, ImageNet-1K does not justify
the stronger claim that each class forms a compact three-coordinate prototype.
The first three displayed components are a communication device, not a complete
description of the high-dimensional semantic representation.

Progress I therefore answers RQ1 within the tested ConvNeXt settings. A learned
late transition can be selected without evaluation leakage, is absent from
architecture-matched random controls, is robust to selector ablation, and is
localized to a major Pre-LN/Post-LN reconditioning event. Replication across
additional architectures and trained seeds remains necessary before claiming a
universal transition location. These findings motivate the next two stages:
\methodname{} uses representation geometry for class-agnostic localization,
while \jmethod{} tests whether selected geometry has transferable training
utility.
